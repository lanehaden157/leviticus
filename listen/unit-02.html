Leviticus, unit 2

Briefing

The second chapter of Leviticus is the shortest of the offering laws, and the only one with no blood in it. Yahweh has just told Moshe how to bring a whole animal to the altar, from the herd, the flock, or among the birds. Now he describes a gift made of grain. Its name is minhah, and this book renders it gift offering. The word is an ordinary one for a present: Jacob sends one ahead to his brother Esau in the book of Genesis, and the same word is used of Abel's offering. Here it narrows to grain, oil, and frankincense.

Notice how the chapter is built. It is a list of ways to give. Raw fine flour, with oil poured on it and frankincense set on top. Or baked in an oven, or toasted on a griddle, or fried in a pan. Or, at the end, green grain from the first harvest, parched in fire. In every case a priest turns the memorial portion into smoke as a fire offering; for the raw and the cooked gifts it is called an aroma of rest to Yahweh, and the last paragraph says only "fire offering". Everything else goes to Aharon and his sons, called most holy, for the raw and cooked gifts; the paragraph on first grain does not say who gets what is left. Scholars have long said this is the offering for someone who cannot afford an animal, and Milgrom says so firmly. That is a reading of where the chapter sits, though. The chapter itself never mentions poverty.

Three words to watch. Memorial portion, in Hebrew azkarah, is rare, and Milgrom admits no theory of it is certain. Is it a reminder for the giver, a fragrance, or a small piece standing for the whole? Salt of the covenant, near the end, ties this kitchen offering to something much larger, because the same phrase will describe the priests' permanent share and the kingdom of David. And leaven and honey, which the altar refuses.

Two tensions to carry along. The priests' share here is given to Aharon and his sons, yet a later chapter gives the baked and fried gifts only to the priest who offers them. And the last paragraph, on first grain, may be a free gift, as Ibn Ezra reads it, or the required sheaf of the barley harvest, as Rashi and Milgrom read it. Neither reading is settled by the text.

This chapter leans on the opening of the book for its refrain. It also looks ahead to the law for the priests, to the harvest festival that sets the first sheaf, and to the poor person's gift in the offering for wrongdoing. Each time you meet the gift offering again, remember that it began here as a handful of flour and a pinch of salt.
