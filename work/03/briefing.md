This is the third of the voluntary offerings. The book's first speech from the tent began with the going up offering, then the grain offering, and now it turns to an animal whose meat, unlike the first, does not all go up in smoke. The Hebrew name is shelamim, and the translation here is well-being offering. Nobody knows quite where the word comes from. Milgrom lists about seven guesses, from peace and wholeness to repayment, covenant and gift, and calls them all educated guesses. The English name is a choice, not a finding.

The chapter is a close cousin of chapter one. Three panels, for the herd, the sheep and the goat, each close with a paragraph break in the Hebrew text. The offerer brings the animal near, leans a hand on its head, and it is slaughtered at the entrance of the tent. The priests dash the blood against the altar. Listen for the verbs and phrases that came back from the first unit: bring near, lean, whole, dash, fire offering, aroma of rest. They are the same, and the differences are worth noticing. Male or female is allowed here. Nothing is skinned or cut up. Only the fat is burned: the fat around the inner organs, the two kidneys, the lobe on the liver, and for the sheep the fat tail. No birds are allowed.

Notice what the chapter does not say. It never says who eats the meat. That comes later, in the instructions for the priests and in the chapter on slaughter outside the camp. Milgrom infers from those places, and from stories such as the sons of Eli at Shiloh, that the point of this offering was a meal the offerer shared, with the fat as the one share that belongs to Yahweh. Morales reads the whole sequence of the first chapters as a journey: cleansing, consecration, and finally fellowship at a table in the presence of God. That is inference. The text gives the acts and leaves the meaning mostly to the reader.

Two things to watch. First, the fat and the parts named for burning are almost word for word the parts burned from the ram of consecration in Exodus, so the everyday offering echoes the priests' ordination. Second, the last sentence, that no one may eat fat or blood, in all your settlements, for all time. Milgrom thinks these lines, at least in part, were added by a later hand, the holiness school, and that they say all table meat first had to be offered. Rashbam, a medieval Jewish reader, and much later Or HaChaim, read it more widely, as covering any animal. Deuteronomy later allows ordinary slaughter and repeats only the ban on blood. How the two fit is left open here.
