The first three offerings were gifts. This passage is different. Yahweh speaks again to Moshe, and the subject is what to do when someone has done wrong without meaning to. The ordering is by rank. First the anointed priest, then the whole community, then a chieftain, then an ordinary person. Each is given an animal, and the more serious the case, the further the blood goes into the Tent, until for the priest and the people it reaches the curtain that hides the innermost room. After the four cases comes a second block. A witness who keeps silent, someone who touches something unclean and forgets, someone who swears rashly and forgets: for these the offering is scaled not to rank but to means, from a lamb or goat down to two birds, and at the very bottom a tenth of an ephah of fine flour.

Hold on to the main word, which the book renders sin offering, the Hebrew chattat. Milgrom, our main commentator, argues the name is misleading. For him it is a purification offering, because the blood is used on the altar and the Tent, not on the person. On his view the wrong leaves a stain on the sanctuary, and the blood cleans it. Ramban reads the same law differently: the stain is on the soul. Rashi, following the Sifra, ties the offering to sins grave enough to carry cutting off. The book keeps sin offering and puts Milgrom's case in notes. Watch the verb cover, kipper, and the closing line, "and he will be forgiven".

Notice the word for unwitting, shegagah, and what it leaves open. Milgrom says the offender knows what he did but not that it was wrong, or the reverse. And notice the first case. The priest's wrong is said to bring guilt on the people. Ibn Ezra, Rashi and Sforno each read that line differently, and the text does not decide.

Two tensions to carry. The bulls whose blood goes into the Tent are burned outside the camp, in a place called clean, while the goat and lamb are eaten by the priests. And the second block mixes things the first does not. There the wrong may be deliberate, and confession is required. Milgrom treats it as its own kind of offering, a graduated one.

Earlier passages to listen for: the ordination bull in Exodus, whose rite is nearly the same, and the Day of Purgation later in this book, which uses the same blood and the same seven sprinklings. Numbers has its own law for unwitting wrong, with a different offering.
