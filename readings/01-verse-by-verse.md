The call

The book opens with a verb, and the verb is the point. Wa-yyiqra, "and he called." That grammar, the "and" at the front, joins Leviticus to the last scene of Exodus: the cloud over the Tent, the glory filling the dwelling, and Moshe unable to go in. So the call is not just a greeting. Moshe is standing outside a house that Yahweh has filled, and he waits to be summoned. Milgrom reads this as the Priestly writers' deliberate point. Even Moshe, who once went up into the cloud on Sinai, does not step into God's presence here; he stands in the court and hears a voice from the Tent. Sforno agrees that Moshe could not enter until he was given leave. Ramban offers two possibilities, that Moshe was afraid, or that he did not yet know where the voice would come from. Ibn Ezra takes a different line and has Moshe go in after the call, past the curtain into the innermost room; Milgrom says the Priestly tradition keeps Moshe out of the innermost room altogether, so Ibn Ezra is reading against it.

Hear the family resemblance. In the book of Exodus, God called to Moshe out of a bush, then out of a mountain, then out of the middle of a cloud. Now he calls from a tent. Each time the place of the call is the place where God is dwelling at that stage of the story. Morales sets these four calls side by side. Rashbam says the same thing in his own way: the voice came from the mountain, and now it comes from the house, because God has taken up residence there. The Tent is a small portable Sinai, and Ramban, quoted by Milgrom, draws out the same parallel: the rules for keeping outsiders away from the Tent repeat the rules for keeping the people away from the mountain.

The speech that follows is a small detail with a large effect. Yahweh says to speak to the children of Israel, not to the priests. Milgrom contrasts this with Mesopotamia and Egypt, where ordinary people were kept away from the temple ritual and even from the texts that described it. Here the ritual is given to everyone. It is a textbook for all Israel, not a secret of the priestly guild.

The verse about the person who brings an offering contains a puzzle that the commentators keep returning to. It speaks of "a person among you," adam, a word that includes men and women and, here, only Israelites. The verb is yaqrib, "brings near," and the noun is qorban, the brought near offering. Both come from the root meaning to come close. In Hebrew the sentence ends oddly with the pronoun "of you," and Ibn Ezra and Milgrom both discuss where that little word belongs. Rashi adds that Scripture says adam, a name that recalls the first man, and he draws the lesson that the first man had nothing stolen to give; neither should you. The animal must be domestic, from the herd, bakar, or the flock, tson, not from wild game.

The herd

The first panel is for a bull, and it is the longest because it must teach the rite. If the offering is an olah, a going up offering, from the herd, it must be a male, zakar, and whole, tamim, a word that means complete, without defect. The book renders it "whole." Milgrom notes that the same word, tamim, runs through the later laws on blemished animals; the prophet Malachi complains in just these terms about the offerers who bring a blind or lame animal and would not dare give it to the governor. The man brings it to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, which Milgrom takes to mean the whole court in front of the Tent. It is there for acceptance, li-retsono, which can also be read "for his acceptance." The word means that the animal, if whole, will win the offerer favor. It is the same verb that Malachi uses when he asks whether the governor will be pleased.

Then the hand. The offerer leans his hand on the head of the animal. The verb samakh means pressing down, and Milgrom shows it is a different word from the gentler verb for resting a hand in blessing. Everyone who has written on this rite has had to decide what it means. One view says the offerer is handing his sin to the animal. Another says he is identifying himself with it. A third, Milgrom's, says he is establishing that it is his. Milgrom's best argument is the single hand: the goat sent off on the Day of Coverings gets both hands, and sins are confessed over it, while the ordinary offering gets one. Ibn Ezra made the same point, and Milgrom builds on it. Ramban answers that the rabbis read both hands for every offering, and that the singular only rules out a stand-in. Morales prefers substitution: the hand says "this animal is me." He argues that a transfer of sin would make the animal unfit to go up as a pleasing offering. Keep both views in mind. The text itself only says that leaning is how the offering becomes acceptable for the offerer, and what it does for him is expressed by the verb kipper.

Kipper is the book's most loaded word, and this is its first use. This study renders it "cover." Milgrom thinks the older sense was to purge or expiate. Ibn Ezra reads it as paying a ransom, and points to the half shekel in the book of Exodus, which was given as a covering for each life. Rashi, following the rabbinic Sifra, says this voluntary offering covers for neglected positive commands. What strikes some readers is that a voluntary gift is described, at its first mention, as doing something about guilt. Milgrom's answer is historical: he thinks the going up offering was originally the main way to cover wrongs, and the sin offering and guilt offering of the next chapters were added later and took that work over. That rests on his reconstruction of the sources, so hold it loosely.

Now the slaughter. The text says "he shall slaughter," and it is the offerer who does it, before Yahweh, meaning in the court. Rashi, Ramban and Milgrom all say a layman's slaughter is valid. The priests take over with the blood. They bring it, and dash it, zaraq, against the altar all around. Blood is not a part of the offering that gets burned; Milgrom says it is the animal's life, returned to God. The altar stands at the entrance of the Tent, and Ibn Ezra and Rashbam say the phrase is there so we do not confuse it with the little altar inside.

The offerer then flays the animal and cuts it into its pieces, nittaḥ. This is a rare verb: about nine verses in the whole Hebrew Bible, among them the matching rite for the ordination ram in Exodus and, later, the dark story at the end of Judges where a body is cut up and sent around the tribes. Do not push that echo, since it is a shared word, not a shared scene. The priests, the sons of Aharon, then do their part: they put fire on the altar, arrange wood, and lay out the pieces, with the head and the fat, on the wood. Ramban noted that the order here, fire after cutting up, does not match the Temple practice, and Milgrom answers that Leviticus is describing a private voluntary offering, not the daily public one.

The innards and the legs are washed, and then the priest turns the whole, ha-kol, into smoke on the altar. The verb is hiqtir, which Milgrom says is deliberately different from the ordinary word for burning: it describes what happens to an offering, a change of state, not destruction. What goes up is "a going up offering, a fire offering, an aroma of rest to Yahweh." The Hebrew is ʾishsheh reaḥ niḥoaḥ, and each phrase carries a decision. Fire offering: Milgrom doubts the word comes from the Hebrew for fire at all and suggests "food gift," because bread and wine, which are not burned, also get that name. Rashi derives it from fire. The book keeps "fire offering." Aroma of rest: the word niḥoaḥ comes from the verb to rest, and the same pair of words describes Noah's offering after the flood, when Yahweh smelled the pleasing aroma and resolved not to curse the ground again. Milgrom thinks the older sense of "soothing" had faded and the Priestly meaning is "pleasing." Morales hears the older sense, "propitiating," and points to Noah, Abraham and David as three moments where a going up offering ends an anger or opens a promise. Ibn Ezra offers both ideas. Rashi gives the gentlest reading: it pleases God that he commanded and his will was done.

The flock

The second panel is short because it says, in effect, "the same, with these changes." The offering may come from sheep or goats, and again must be a male, whole. Here we learn where the animal is slaughtered: on the north side of the altar. Milgrom thinks the bull and the flock animals were killed in different places, and gives a practical reason: the east was the ash heap, the west the basin, the south the ramp, and the north was left. The panel leaves out the hand and the flaying because the herd panel already said that these applied to every going up offering. The Greek translation adds the hand-leaning back into this verse. The ending has the same refrain, an aroma of rest to Yahweh.

The birds

The third panel begins differently, and the difference is noted by scholars as a clue. The offering is from birds: turtledoves, torim, or young pigeons, bene yonah. There is no word about a male or about a whole animal; Milgrom thinks the poor are being given a way in, and that the requirements were waived because a bird's sex and blemishes cannot easily be checked. He tells a story from the Midrash of a king who wanted to be the only offerer on a certain day, and a poor man came with two doves. Ramban offers a reason for the choice of birds: turtledoves stay faithful to their mates, and young pigeons stay with the nest. A reader may or may not hear that as the meaning of the text.

The rite is different because the bird is small. The priest brings it to the altar himself, since the offerer's hand carries it; Milgrom suggests this is why the hand-leaning is dropped. He wrings off the head, malaq, a verb found only here and in the sin offering of birds, and turns it into smoke; the blood is drained against the wall of the altar. Then he removes the crop, or whatever the Hebrew word means, together with its feathers, and throws it beside the altar, to the east, where the ashes are. This is the one unsettled detail in the chapter. The word is murʾa. Rashi takes it as the crop together with the entrails, and also passes on the rabbis' reading, the crop with the feathers on the skin around it. Ramban objects to Rashi's own reading and sides with the feathers. Milgrom argues, with the help of an experienced taxidermist, that it is the area around the vent, removed by cutting through the tail and used as a handle to pull out the digestive tract. We say that the translation here is uncertain.

Last, the priest tears it by its wings, bi-kenapayw, but does not divide it. The wings are left on, Milgrom says, to make the small bird look like a larger gift. That "does not divide it" has an old echo. When Abraham was told to bring a heifer, a goat, a ram, a turtledove and a young pigeon, he cut the animals in halves but not the birds. Those are exactly the five creatures of the going up offering in this chapter, and the birds go whole in both places.

The last verse closes the chapter with the same refrain, an aroma of rest to Yahweh. Nothing in the chapter says why a person might bring such a gift. Later in the book some offerings will be demanded for particular wrongs, and for those the cases will be spelled out. This chapter has none of that. It gives the form, and leaves the reasons to the person. For later readers, the New Testament letters draw on this vocabulary, in the letter to the Hebrews, where Christ's body replaces the offerings, and in Paul's picture of the believer as a living sacrifice. Those are readings that start from Leviticus, not meanings Leviticus gives itself.
