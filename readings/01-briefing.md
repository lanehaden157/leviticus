This is the first unit of Leviticus, and it opens with a door that has just been shut. The book of Exodus ends with the cloud covering the Tent of Meeting and Yahweh's glory filling the dwelling, so fully that Moshe cannot go in. Leviticus begins on the very next breath. The first Hebrew word means "and he called," and what it picks up is that closed door. Yahweh has come to live among his people, and no one, not even Moshe, can yet come near him. The sacrifices of this chapter are the way that nearness begins.

Notice who speaks, and from where. Yahweh calls to Moshe from the Tent. Earlier, he had called to Moshe from a bush, from a mountain, and from inside a cloud. Now he calls from a tent, a house pitched in the middle of the camp. The first thing he says from it is not a law about the priests. It is a set of instructions for any Israelite who wants to bring a gift.

That word, gift, is worth holding onto. The Hebrew word for what is brought is qorban, from the verb meaning to bring near, and this book renders it brought near offering. The offering is what you bring near because you want to come near yourself. And this first offering is voluntary. Nobody here is making amends for a particular wrong. A person wants to give something to God and is told how.

The offering in this chapter is the olah, which this study calls the going up offering. The name comes from the verb to go up. It is the one offering that is turned entirely into smoke on the altar and so, in a sense, goes up whole. Scholars still argue about whether the older sense of the word was "whole" or "burnt," and about what it was for. Milgrom, our main commentator, thinks it began as a general gift that could also cover sin, before the later sin and guilt offerings took that work over. Morales, reading the book as a story of ascent into God's presence, takes the name to mean that the worshipper himself goes up through the animal.

Watch for the rhythm. The chapter is built as three parallel panels, one for an animal from the herd, one for the flock, and one for birds, so that even the poor can bring something. The herd panel gives the full sequence: bring it near, lean a hand on its head, slaughter it, let the priests dash the blood on the altar, cut it up, wash the inner parts, and turn it all into smoke. The flock panel repeats it in short form, and the bird panel keeps the same frame but changes the steps to suit a small animal. Each panel ends with the same refrain, a fire offering, an aroma of rest to Yahweh. The repetition is the point. This is a chapter you learn by hearing it again.

Some words to watch. The verb translated lean is samakh, which means to press down, not just to rest a hand. What the pressing means is debated. Is it handing over one's sin? Claiming the animal as one's own? Declaring, "this animal is me"? You will meet each of those answers, and the chapter itself never says. The verb translated cover is kipper, which most translations render as atone. This book uses cover, and the commentators split on what the covering does: make atonement, purge, pay a ransom. And the phrase aroma of rest echoes the offering Noah made after the flood.

There are tensions worth keeping open. The chapter says the offering is "for acceptance," and also "to cover for him." Is this offering a gift, or is it also dealing with sin? It tells the offerer to do the slaughtering, yet the priests handle the blood. Who is a layman allowed to be near, and where? And the bird panel looks like a later addition to some scholars and like the poor person's door to others.

Finally, the passages this unit leans on. The ordination of the priests in Exodus, where the same sequence is carried out on a ram. The offering at the entrance of the Tent in Exodus, the daily one, where Yahweh says he will meet his people. And Noah's offering. Listen for those as the chapter runs.
