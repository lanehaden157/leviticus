The Book of Leviticus · Study Translation

The gift offering

Unit 2 · Leviticus 2:1–16

    The gift from raw flour · 2:1–3

    1 And a person, when he brings near a brought near offering, a gift offering to Yahweh: fine flour shall his brought near offering be. And he shall pour oil on it and put frankincense on it.12

    a person is nepesh, a word used of men and women alike: any Israelite may bring this. The offerer does little: pours the oil, sets the frankincense and brings it.
    Mal 1:11. A prophet says that a pure gift offering is brought near to Yahweh's name in every place. Tentative: the link is the shared word for the gift offering, nothing more. Later readers took the verse to mean that this cheap gift, within anyone's reach, stands as high as an animal.

    2 And he shall bring it to Aharon's sons, the priests, and scoop out from there his handful of its fine flour and of its oil, along with all its frankincense; and the priest shall turn into smoke its reminder portion on the altar: a fire offering, an aroma of rest to Yahweh.3

    One priest takes one handful; it stands in for the whole gift. The rest of the flour is not burned.
    Lev 5:11–13. When a poor person cannot afford even two birds for a sin offering, a tenth of an ephah of fine flour is allowed, with no oil and no frankincense; the priest takes the same handful as its reminder portion and turns it into smoke. The shared words are the fine flour, the handful, the reminder portion and the turning into smoke; the difference is that the oil and frankincense are left out there, since that gift answers a wrong, not a free gift.
    Num 5:15–26. The jealousy trial uses the same handful and the same reminder portion, with barley flour, no oil and no frankincense. The rite here is a joyful gift; there the same acts serve a suspicion. What is shared is the form of the act, not its purpose.

    3 And the remainder of the gift offering shall be Aharon's and his sons': most holy, from the fire offerings of Yahweh.

    most holy is literally "holy of holies": the priests eat this portion, and only they.
    Lev 6:14–18; 10:12–13. The priests' own instructions say how the remainder is eaten: without leaven, in a holy place, by the males of Aharon's house. On the day his sons die, Moshe tells Aharon to eat it beside the altar, so the rule is used the first time it is needed.

    The baked and cooked gifts · 2:4–12

    Oven, griddle and pan · 2:4–7

    The same fine flour may also be brought near baked, and three baked forms are named. From the oven: unleavened cakes mixed with oil, or unleavened wafers anointed with oil. On a griddle: unleavened fine flour mixed with oil, broken into bits with more oil poured on it. In a pan: fine flour made with oil. Each is called a gift offering.4

    To the priest and the altar · 2:8–10

    Whatever is made of these, the offerer brings to Yahweh and presents to the priest, who takes it to the altar. The priest lifts off its reminder portion and turns it into smoke, a fire offering, an aroma of rest to Yahweh; the remainder is Aharon's and his sons', most holy, from the fire offerings of Yahweh.9

    No leaven, no honey · 2:11–12

    No gift offering that y'all bring near to Yahweh is to be made with leavened dough, for y'all shall not turn into smoke leaven or honey as a fire offering. As a first-processed brought near offering y'all may bring them near to Yahweh, but they shall not go up to the altar as an aroma of rest.56

    Salt · 2:13

    13 And every brought near offering of your gift offering you shall season with salt; and you shall not let the salt of the covenant of your God rest from upon your gift offering. On every brought near offering of yours you shall bring near salt.7

    The speech turns to one person: you, singular, the offerer. Rest is the verb of the Sabbath and the aroma, here said of salt, which must not "rest" away.
    Num 18:19. The salt of the covenant is said again, of the portions that go to the priests: a lasting statute, a salt covenant before Yahweh. The words are the same, and in both places they point to something that does not lapse.
    Exod 30:35. The holy incense is "salted," with the same rare root as the verb here. Tentative: the only other place in the Torah that uses the root, a link of the same sanctuary and the same frankincense.

    The first-ripe grain · 2:14–16

    Fresh heads, parched · 2:14–16

    If y'all bring near a gift offering of first-ripe grain, it is fresh heads of grain parched with fire and crushed. Oil and frankincense are put on it, and it is a gift offering. The priest turns into smoke its reminder portion, from the crushed grain and the oil with all the frankincense: a fire offering to Yahweh.8

    Notes

    1. fine flour (v1). solet, the grade of flour that makes the gift. Some take it as semolina, coarse wheat grits; that rests on later sayings about the sieve and on cognates in other languages, not on this verse. The English "fine flour" keeps to what the Hebrew word is plainly used for.
    2. gift offering (v1). minḥah is a gift in the wider sense: Cain and Abel bring one (Gen 4:3). "Cereal offering" and "meal offering" name what it is made of; the Hebrew names it only a gift. Some translate "tribute," as to a king.
    3. reminder portion (v2). ʾazkarah comes from the root "remember." Readings differ on what it does. Some say it recalls the offerer before Yahweh; some say it is the part whose fragrance rises and does that work; some call it a token portion standing for the whole. Here it is kept as the handful, a "reminder," and none of these is imposed. The word is rare: Num 5:26 and the showbread of Lev 24:7 are its nearest uses.
    4. oven, griddle, pan (vv4–7). tannur, "oven," is a baking pot heated from within; maḥabat, "griddle," a flat plate; marḥeshet, "pan," a deeper vessel, probably for frying. "Cakes" are ḥallot and "wafers" reqiqim. The verb in v6, "break into bits," is found only here in the Bible. The frankincense is mentioned only for the raw flour and the first-ripe grain; whether it is meant for the baked gifts is left unsaid, and it is not assumed here. Exod 29:2; Num 6:15. The cakes and wafers of the oven gift are the ones in the ordination basket, and again in the Nazirite's closing offering: fine flour, unleavened, mixed or anointed with oil, in nearly the same words. So the baked gifts here are the same bread of Yahweh's house seen at its two other great moments.
    5. leaven and honey (v11). ḥamets is "leavened" dough or bread; śeʾor is the leaven that makes it rise. "Honey" is debash, which covers bee honey and also fruit or date syrup; the text does not choose. No reason for the ban is given. It is common to read leaven as fermentation and so decay, honey as the same, and the pair as unfit for the altar; that is a reading and rests on the later use of leaven as an image. Exod 12:15; 13:7. Leaven and leavened bread are the pair Yisrael was to remove from its houses at the exodus. In both places it is a matter of what is kept out of Yahweh's presence: out of the houses for the festival, off the altar here. 1 Cor 5:6–8; Luke 12:1. A little leaven in the whole lump, and "the leaven of the Pharisees," are the New Testament's pictures of corruption that spreads. This is reception: Leviticus gives no reason for the ban, and the later use does not say what the verse meant.
    6. first-processed (v12). qorban reʾshit is not the same as bikkurim, "first-ripe," of v14. The two words are kept apart: reʾshit is the first part taken from the harvest, here the processed grain, and bikkurim the first ripe produce. English Bibles usually write "firstfruits" for both. Num 18:12–13 uses the pair again, which is the main support for keeping them apart. Lev 7:13; 23:17. Leavened bread is brought near with the thanksgiving offering, and two leavened loaves are brought near as firstfruits. Leaven is not forbidden to Yahweh's house as such, only to the altar's fire. Verse 12 says this: what is brought near as a first-processed gift is not turned into smoke.
    7. salt (v13). The verb timlaḥ is "salt," from the same root as melaḥ, "salt." tashbit is "cause to rest": the salt must not stop. "The salt of the covenant" is not explained here; Num 18:19 uses the phrase for what is lasting.
    8. fresh heads, parched and crushed (v14). ʾabib qaluy ba-ʾesh geres karmel: the words are rare and the sense is unsettled. Some take geres as "broken, crushed" grain and karmel as "fresh"; others read "milky grain," the soft grain of the ear before it hardens; the Greek reads "new grain, roasted and ground." The rendering here follows a plain reading of the parts: heads of grain, parched in the fire and crushed. Lev 23:9–14. The sheaf of firstfruits is brought before Yahweh, and no bread, parched grain or fresh grain may be eaten before it. The word for fresh grain is the one used here. Whether 2:14 is that sheaf of the barley harvest, or a gift anyone may bring at any time, is argued. Josh 5:11; 2 Kgs 4:42. Yisrael eats parched grain the day after the Passover when it crosses into the land, and a man brings Elisha bread of firstfruits and fresh ears. Tentative: both share a rare word of this verse; neither says it is this offering.
    9. who eats the remainder (vv8–10). Lev 7:9–10. Another rule seems to give the baked, griddle and pan gifts to the one priest who brings them near, and the dry or oiled gifts to all of Aharon's sons. Verse 10 here gives the remainder to Aharon and his sons. How the two fit is open: the whole body of priests may share, or the rules speak of different cases. Tentative: neither passage says it resolves the other.
